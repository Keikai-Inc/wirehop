Comparison

WireHop vs Tailscale
for AI agents

Tailscale is a good product with a bigger ecosystem, a real admin console, and years more polish. If you want a managed control plane and single sign-on, use it. This page is about the one thing it can't do, and why that matters if your agents are doing the work.

The actual difference Where Tailscale is better

One architectural difference

Everything else on this page follows from it.

Tailscale runs a coordination server: a hosted service that knows your devices, holds your account, and hands out the keys that let machines find each other. Your traffic is end-to-end encrypted and doesn't flow through it, but your membership does, and getting a device onto the network means authenticating to it.

WireHop has no such service. A machine's identity is a keypair written to disk the first time it runs, and membership is a document your machines replicate among themselves. Joining means redeeming a token minted by a machine you already control.

For a person, that difference is mostly philosophical. For an agent, it's the difference between finishing and stopping: an account is a step only a human can complete.

Pick the right one

We would rather you use the tool that fits than churn a setup that already works.

Use Tailscale if

  • You're connecting people in an organization and want SSO, groups, and provisioning
  • You need Windows, iOS, or Android clients
  • Someone needs a web console to see and approve devices
  • You want centralized audit streamed into a SIEM
  • You want a published third-party security audit
  • Your NAT situation is unusual and you want the most battle-tested traversal

Use WireHop if

  • You want an AI agent to build and operate the network without you
  • You want nobody in the middle: no account, no service that could be breached or shut down
  • You're connecting your own machines, not an org of users
  • You want shell, file sync, fleet commands and scheduling in the same tool as the network
  • You'd rather assign a role than author a policy file

They also coexist. WireHop notices when the 100.64.0.0/10 address range is already claimed by another interface and skips bringing up its own network rather than fighting over it. Reaching machines, running commands, and copying files keep working either way.
